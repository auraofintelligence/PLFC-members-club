First aid and emergencies

Someone in danger?

Call now. Put the phone on speaker and follow the operator. Give the island name, beach or jetty, nearest access point and what happened. At sea, use VHF channel 16 for distress.

Shark bite or life-threatening bleeding

Blood pouring or spurting, a rapidly growing pool of blood, or part of a limb torn off needs immediate action.

  1. Get help and press hard. Once on safe ground, lie the person down. Have someone call 000 for an ambulance while you press a pad, cloth or your hand firmly over the bleeding point. If alone, call on speaker and press. Keep pressing. If something is embedded, press on either side of it.
  2. If pressure fails on an arm or leg, use an arterial tourniquet. If trained and a commercial tourniquet is available, follow its instructions. Without placement instructions, fit it about 5 cm above the bleeding point, towards the body, clear of the wound and any joint. Tighten until the bleeding stops. If you are unfamiliar with the device, tell 000 and follow their directions.
  3. Note the time and leave it in place. Keep the tourniquet visible. Never loosen or remove it yourself. If bleeding continues, check the fit and tell 000. A second tourniquet may be needed above the first. Give the ambulance crew the application time.

For the neck, chest, belly or groin, keep pressure on the bleeding point and follow 000. A limb tourniquet goes on an arm or leg only. Keep the person warm and watch their breathing.

Rip currents and drowning

Caught in a rip?

Float to conserve energy. Raise one arm and call for help. Swimming against the current can exhaust you. If able, swim across the current towards breaking waves, then reassess.

Someone else is in trouble?

Alert a lifesaver or call 000. From a safe position, throw something that floats. Stay out of the water if you lack rescue training or suitable equipment.

Once on dry land

Unresponsive and not breathing normally, including no breathing or only gasps? Start CPR. Press the centre of the chest 100 to 120 times a minute. Give 30 compressions then 2 rescue breaths if able. Breaths matter after drowning. If unable to give breaths, continue compressions. Send someone for an AED, start CPR while they fetch it and follow the device’s prompts.

Unresponsive but breathing normally? Roll the person onto their side, keep the airway clear and recheck breathing. Call an ambulance for anyone who needed treatment after drowning, even if they seem well again. Anyone who had breathing trouble after submersion needs medical assessment.

Bluebottles, jellyfish, stingrays and fish spines

Get out of the water. Call 000 for collapse, breathing trouble, widespread or severe symptoms, a suspected dangerous jellyfish sting or a barb in the chest or belly.

Recognisable bluebottle sting

Gently rinse with sea water and lift off remaining tentacles. Avoid rubbing, fresh water and vinegar. For pain, use hot water the person can comfortably tolerate for 20 minutes. Check the temperature on unaffected skin first to avoid a burn.

Unsure what stung them?

Treatment depends on the animal. Tell the lifesaver what happened or ring the Queensland Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26. For serious symptoms, call 000 first.

Possible blue-ringed octopus bite or cone shell sting

Call 000 immediately, even if the bite barely hurts. Keep the person lying still. For a bitten arm or leg, apply a firm pressure immobilisation bandage over the bite and along the limb, then splint it. This bandage should allow circulation. Follow the operator and watch breathing closely.

Stingray barb or fish spine

Leave an embedded spine in place. For bleeding, pad around it and press beside it. Get urgent medical assessment. Once serious bleeding is controlled, a stung hand or foot can be immersed in tolerably hot water for pain while arranging help.

Fishhook in skin or near an eye

Only the tip has pricked the skin

Stop the line pulling. If the barb is completely outside the skin and the tip is superficial, gently ease the tip out along the way it went in. Stop if it catches. Rinse with clean drinking water and cover with a clean dressing.

The barb is buried, or you are unsure

Keep the hook and lure still. Cover spare hook points and seek medical care for removal. Pulling on a buried barb tears tissue. Tell the clinician if the hook was in sea water and ask about tetanus protection.

In or near the eye? Leave the hook in place, keep pressure off the eye and call 000. Seek urgent care for a deep hook or one near a joint, tendon or major blood vessel.

Slips, trips, falls and head injuries

Check the rocks, swell, boat deck or traffic before approaching. Ask where it hurts. If they may have injured their head, neck or back, keep them still and call 000. Move them only for immediate danger or to protect the airway. Normal breathing takes priority.

After a head knock, loss of consciousness, a seizure, repeated vomiting, worsening confusion or new weakness needs emergency help. For a painful or deformed limb, support it where it lies and seek assessment.

Cuts from knives, shells or rocks

Press a clean pad firmly over the cut until bleeding stops. For a small cut, rinse with clean drinking water or sterile saline, then cover with a clean dressing. Leave embedded objects in place and press around them.

Seek medical care promptly for a deep, gaping, dirty or puncture wound. Mention sea water exposure and ask about tetanus. Spreading redness, swelling, fever or worsening pain needs medical attention. For heavy ongoing bleeding, use the major bleeding steps.

BBQ, engine and hot-water burns

Make the area safe. Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. Remove rings, watches and loose clothing before swelling starts. Leave stuck clothing in place.

Cover loosely with a clean, non-stick dressing. Keep the rest of the person warm. Avoid ice, butter or creams. Call 000 for a large or deep burn, breathing trouble or suspected inhalation injury. Burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals or major joints need urgent medical care.

Sunscreen, sunburn and overheating

Before fishing

When UV is 3 or above, use shade, a broad-brimmed hat, sunglasses and clothing that covers your skin. Apply broad-spectrum, water-resistant SPF 50 or 50+ sunscreen 20 minutes before going outside. Reapply at least every two hours and after swimming, sweating or towel drying.

Sunburn or heat exhaustion

Get into shade, cool the skin with water and remove excess layers. If the person is alert and swallowing normally, offer cool water. Get medical advice for extensive blistering, vomiting or symptoms that persist.

Confusion, collapse or a seizure in the heat? Suspect heatstroke. Call 000 and cool them immediately with water, wet towels and fanning. Keep their airway clear. Give nothing to drink if drowsy or confused.
